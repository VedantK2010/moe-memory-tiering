CXL-based memory optimisation for MoE models

Expert tiering
across HBM and CXL

Mixture-of-Experts models keep eight FFN experts per layer resident but touch only two per token. That is an invitation to demote the cold ones to CXL-attached memory — if the placement policy is good enough to keep the hot ones local. We measured 1.66 million real Mixtral 8x7B routing decisions against four policies and a cycle-accurate DRAM model. The best policy is not the same one at every layer.

0x20

Does placement intelligence actually pay?

"Less CXL is faster" is true no matter which experts you pick, so it proves nothing. The honest test holds the capacity budget fixed and asks whether a hot/cold ranking beats an arbitrary one. Against 20 random placements per budget, frequency-ranked placement wins at every non-trivial budget, peaking where the decision is hardest — six of eight slots.

Depth changes which policy wins

This is the finding we did not expect. We pulled every routing decision from two layers of the allenai/analysis_mixtral trace — layer 15 in the middle of the stack, layer 31 at the top — and ran the same three policies on both. The ordering inverts.

Middle layers route on context

At layer 15 the identity of the hot expert turns over quickly with the subject matter. A frozen top-4 is stale within a few thousand tokens, which is why static bottoms out at 57.5% while LRU, which re-decides on every access, reaches 64.9%. Re-profiling every 2,000 tokens lands between them — it adapts, but on a delay.

Deep layers route on syntax

At layer 31 routing concentrates on a stable handful of experts, so a frozen top-4 is already close to optimal: static reaches 65.8% and LRU beats it by 0.15 points — inside the noise. Periodic re-profiling wins here precisely because it is less reactive: it keeps a good set instead of churning it.

0x40

When the workload shifts underneath you

To isolate adaptation from everything else, we built a three-phase synthetic trace whose hot experts rotate at each boundary, and gave the static policy the deal a real deployment gets: profile once on phase 0, then live with it. No look-ahead for anyone.

Average access time per phase

Synthetic HBM budget = 4

Static's collapse is the whole story. It is the fastest policy in phase 0 — it was tuned on phase 0 — and the slowest by phase 2, degrading 12.5% as the distribution rotates away from it. Periodic re-profiling is the only policy that is never worst in any phase, which is what makes it the best overall choice despite never being the best in phase 0.

0x50

How often should you re-profile?

Re-profiling is not free — every re-rank implies migrating experts across the link. Too frequent and you chase noise in a short window; too rare and you are running a stale map. The curve has a clear floor at roughly 2,000 tokens between re-ranks.

Re-profiling interval sweep

log scale · lower is better

At a 2,000-token cadence the policy beats all three fixed alternatives. Pushed out to 20,000 tokens it is worse than doing nothing — it has become static placement with extra migration traffic. The window has to be short enough to catch a phase change and long enough to estimate a ranking from.

0x60

Energy follows the hit rate

A CXL fetch crosses a PCIe Gen5 PHY, and the PHY is where the joules go. Charging CXL at roughly 3× an equivalent local HBM fetch, total memory energy tracks hit rate almost exactly — which means the policy choice at layer 15 is worth about 8% of the memory power budget.

The ratio is sound; the absolute is not. Our 21.08 nJ/fetch constant comes from DRAMSim3 total energy divided by request count, so it carries the simulator's background and refresh power amortised over the run — it is energy-per-request-in-that-window, not the marginal cost of one 64 B burst. Every percentage on this page is a ratio of two figures built from the same constant, so the constant cancels and the savings hold. The millijoule column does not transfer to silicon.

Does the ranking survive a second dataset?

Every result above rests on particular random draws. We regenerated an independent trace — different seed, sharper skew (α=3 rather than 6), different locality target — and re-ran all four policies. One policy holds its rank; one does not.

Hybrid is the one that breaks. It goes from 2nd to 4th. Sweeping its reserved/LRU split on dataset 2 shows why: the best split there is 0 reserved — that is, pure LRU. The fixed 50/50 ratio was tuned on dataset 1 and does not transfer, so we report the hybrid as a negative result rather than dropping it. Periodic re-profiling is 1st on both.

Method, and what it does not show

The simplifications below are load-bearing. Each one is a place where a reviewer should discount the result, and each is something the next version of this work should close.

Tier model
Access time is latency + size/bandwidth, with HBM at 150 ns / 800 GB/s and CXL at 220 ns / 64 GB/s. These are defensible published ballparks, not a specific part's datasheet numbers.
DRAM validation
256,000 burst commands through DRAMSim3 on an HBM2 config: 60.71 ns mean burst latency, 88.08% row-buffer hit rate. The high row-buffer rate is what justifies treating a bulk expert fetch as a near-streaming transfer in the analytic model.
Trace
1.66 M recorded top-1 routing decisions from allenai/analysis_mixtral, split across layers 15 and 31 (829,441 tokens each). Synthetic traces are used only where a controlled distribution shift is needed.
No migration cost
A cache miss is charged the CXL read but not the cost of installing the expert into HBM, nor the eviction write-back. This flatters every adaptive policy — LRU most of all, since it migrates most often — and is the single largest correction the model needs.
Per-token re-fetch
Each access is charged a full expert transfer. Real inference reads a resident expert's weights at tile granularity without re-fetching, so absolute latencies here are far above a real decode step. Ratios between policies are unaffected; absolute microsecond figures are not comparable to measured inference.
Expert size
16 MiB is a placeholder for address-space layout. Mixtral's real per-expert FFN is 3 × 4096 × 14336 ≈ 176 M parameters — about 336 MiB at fp16, roughly 21× larger. The configurator lets you substitute it.
Top-1 only
The real-trace analysis tracks the first-choice expert. Mixtral routes top-2, so true HBM pressure is higher than modelled and all hit rates here are optimistic.
Energy constant
21.08 nJ/fetch is DRAMSim3 total energy over request count and includes amortised background power; CXL's 3× multiplier is a published PHY ballpark, not a measurement. Relative savings are sound; absolute joules are not.
One model family
Everything is Mixtral 8x7B, 8 experts, top-2. Models with 64 or 128 finer-grained experts (DeepSeek-V3, Qwen-MoE) have flatter routing distributions, where cache-based tiering should behave differently. Untested here.
Prefetching
The first-order Markov prefetcher is a prototype on two traces. It charges nothing for a wrong prediction, although a wrong prefetch consumes real CXL bandwidth. Treat its numbers as an upper bound.

What would change the conclusion. Adding a migration penalty proportional to expert size would penalise LRU hardest and could plausibly reverse the layer-15 result, handing it to periodic re-profiling — whose whole appeal is bounded migration traffic. That experiment is the obvious next one, and it is not yet run.
